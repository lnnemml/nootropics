# NORA Journal — Writing Rules

> **Read this before generating any journal article.**
> This file governs voice, structure, SEO strategy, and content taxonomy
> for all files under `/content/journal/`. It is the authoritative source.
> The NORA Journal is an editorial research publication. Articles that read
> as advertising undermine the strategy. Articles that read as genuine
> research notes build trust that compounds over time.

---

## Tone of voice

### The persona

The author is someone who works inside the nootropics and cognitive
performance space and has done the actual reading — not a science
communicator simplifying things for a general audience, and not a
copywriter framing research to sell something. A researcher who knows
where to look, what a real effect size means, and when a study is too
small to conclude anything.

This person treats the reader as a peer. Not "let me explain this to
you." More: "here's what I found when I dug into this, and here's where
I'm still not satisfied with the evidence."

The authority is epistemic — it comes from depth of reading, not from
credentials or from having a product in the market. When the author says
"the primary endpoint in this trial was physician-assessed, not objective
cognitive measurement," the reader trusts it because it's a specific and
verifiable observation, not a general claim.

### What this sounds like in practice

The writing is direct. No hedges that don't add information ("it may be
the case that," "it could potentially," "some people suggest"). State
what the evidence shows and what it doesn't.

The writing has opinions. Not neutrally presenting every side. Not "some
researchers believe X, while others believe Y." If the evidence points
somewhere, point there. If it's genuinely unclear, say so plainly: "I
don't have a clean answer here."

The writing is specific. Not "research shows improved cognition." Instead:
"The 2007 Kudrin paper found increased tyrosine hydroxylase expression in
dopaminergic neurons after bromantane administration — that's the mechanism
claim. Whether that translates to the subjective motivation effect users
describe is the part we're inferring, not measuring directly."

The writing acknowledges limits without apologizing for them. Not a
litany of disclaimers at the end. Woven in naturally: "This is a rat
study. That matters for how much weight you give it." "The sample was
n=40, one lab, not replicated. Promising, not conclusive."

The writing is occasionally personal. Not "I synthesized this molecule"
but "I've read enough forum threads on this cycling question to know
when a pattern is real versus noise." First person where it clarifies
whose observation this is. Third person or passive where the data speaks
for itself.

### Register

Not academic — no passive voice, no citation-in-brackets-every-sentence,
no section abstracts. Not wellness blog — no "transform your performance,"
no softening of bad news, no promising outcomes. Not Reddit forum — no
hedging everything into "YMMV," no excessive epistemic humility.

The closest single-sentence description: a well-read person who has
been in this space long enough to have opinions, and isn't afraid to
hold them while still being honest about what they don't know.

## Commercial bias rule

NORA sells bromantane. Every reader who finds these articles will
eventually know that. The question is whether they find out from us,
upfront, or from inference later.

When writing articles that directly involve bromantane or NeuroDrive:

**Acknowledge the conflict early, as a plain fact.** Not an apology.
Not excessive qualification. One sentence: "We sell this compound, so
take the enthusiasm with appropriate grain of salt — here's what the
data actually shows." Then proceed to write the most honest analysis
you can, including limitations and competing evidence.

This acknowledgment increases trust. It signals that the journal isn't
a dressed-up sales channel. A reader who sees us honestly cover
bromantane's evidence gaps will believe us more on everything else.

When writing about other compounds — modafinil, racetams, rhodiola,
lion's mane, caffeine — write as if there is no financial stake in the
outcome, because there isn't. NORA's credibility on bromantane is partly
built by being fair about the alternatives.

## Structure and flow

### What not to do

- Symmetric sections of equal length — some sections are two paragraphs
  because that's all the content that exists; don't pad to match others
- Summary paragraph at the end of every H2 restating what was just said
- "In conclusion," "To summarize," "As we've seen," "As we've covered"
- "Now that we've explored X, let's turn to Y" — transition narration
- "It's worth noting that" / "It's important to remember that" / "Notably,"
- "Ultimately" to open the final paragraph
- "In today's world" / "In recent years" / "In today's fast-paced world"
- Three examples where one specific example makes the same point better
- Numbered lists of five or more items where prose reads more naturally
- Rhetorical question answered in the very next sentence — predictable;
  either let it breathe before answering or don't ask it
- "More research is needed" as a conclusion — say what specific research
  would actually change the answer

### What makes good structure

Some sections are short — two paragraphs — because there is nothing more
to say about that point. This is correct. Don't pad.

Some paragraphs don't conclude cleanly because the evidence doesn't. Let
them hang. "I'm not sure what to make of that either."

Sentences vary in length. Sometimes a single short sentence lands harder
than a long one. Sometimes a thought requires thirty words to hold its
precision and cutting it in half loses the meaning. Both are correct
in their context.

Starting a paragraph with "And" or "But" is normal English. Do it when
the paragraph is genuinely continuing or countering what came before.

One digression per article that earns its place. Flag it: "This is a
tangent, but it matters for how you read the next section." Then come
back.

Leave unresolved questions open: "I don't have a clean answer here" is
a complete sentence when it's true.

## SEO rules

### The primary goal

Every journal article should rank for at least one specific search query
within six months of publication. The Journal's traffic is organic —
earned through matching search intent with genuinely useful content,
not through keyword-stuffing or thin coverage.

NORA's domain authority is new. This means competing on head terms
("nootropics," "biohacking") is a losing strategy initially. Win on
long-tail, specific queries first: "how does bromantane work," "what is
an actoprotector," "why does modafinil stop working," "rhodiola rosea
evidence review."

### Search intent — match it exactly

Every article has one primary search intent. Name it before writing.

**Informational intent** — the reader wants to understand something.
Mechanism and research articles. Lead with the answer, then the depth.
If the query is "what is an actoprotector," the first paragraph defines
it clearly. Don't bury the definition three sections in.

**Investigational intent** — the reader is evaluating whether something
is worth their time. "Does lion's mane actually work." "Is bromantane
safe." Don't hedge indefinitely — give a direct answer with the evidence
that supports it and the caveats that qualify it.

**Comparative intent** — "modafinil vs adrafinil," "rhodiola vs ashwagandha."
Structure the article to answer the comparison directly. Don't just
review each in isolation.

**How-to intent** — guide articles. Give the actual framework or protocol
early. Explanation can follow the answer, not precede it.

### Keyword placement

For each article, identify:
- **Primary keyword** — the exact phrase the target reader would type.
  Should appear in: title, meta description, first 100 words, at least
  one H2 subheading (naturally, not forced), and the URL slug.
- **Secondary keywords** — related phrases and synonyms. Use naturally
  in body text. No need to force frequency.
- **Semantic field** — related terms Google expects in a quality article
  on this topic. If writing about dopamine, "tyrosine hydroxylase,"
  "dopaminergic," "reward circuitry," "motivation" should appear
  naturally. Their absence signals thin coverage.

Keyword density: there is no target percentage. Write for the reader.
The primary keyword should appear enough that the topic is clear but
never so many times that a human would notice.

### Titles and meta descriptions

Title format that performs well for this content type:
- "What Is [X]? [Differentiating clause that signals depth]"
- "[X] vs [Y]: What the Evidence Actually Shows"
- "How [X] Works — And What That Means for [Outcome]"
- "The Problem with [Common Approach]: [Specific Reason]"
- "Does [X] Actually Work? Reading the Research"

Avoid:
- Click-bait ("You Won't Believe What This Compound Does")
- Vague ("A Guide to Better Focus")
- Over-optimized ("Bromantane Nootropic Dopamine Focus Brain 2024")

Meta description: one sentence, under 160 characters, written to
answer the search query directly. Not a teaser. Not clever. Direct.
"How bromantane upregulates dopamine synthesis — and why that distinction
from stimulant mechanisms matters for long-term cognitive performance."

### Structure for featured snippets

When the target query is definitional ("what is an actoprotector"), put
a clean 40–60 word definition in the first or second paragraph.
Google pulls this for featured snippets.

When the query is question-based ("does rhodiola reduce cortisol"),
make one H2 that asks or states that question directly, followed by
a direct answer in the first sentence of that section.

For comparison articles, a simple comparison table (rendered in MDX)
near the top helps both readers and search engines.

### Article length by category

- **`mechanism`** — 1,500–2,500 words. Comprehensive on the mechanism,
  honest about evidence gaps. Going shorter risks thin coverage;
  going longer risks padding.
- **`research`** — 1,200–2,000 words. Focused on one body of evidence.
  Don't try to cover everything — cover the specific question well.
- **`perspective`** — 800–1,500 words. These are argued positions.
  Shorter is fine if the argument is complete.
- **`guide`** — 1,000–2,000 words. Give the framework early,
  then depth. Don't bury the useful part in page 3.

### Internal linking

Each article links to 2–3 other journal articles where genuinely
relevant — not forced. If writing about bromantane mechanism, link to
the actoprotector overview article. If writing about dopamine tone,
link to the mechanism article on tyrosine hydroxylase.

One contextual link per article to the NeuroDrive product page is
acceptable — but only where it makes sense in the text, never as an
appended "learn more" at the end. Example: "...which is the mechanism
NeuroDrive's bromantane delivers sublingual rather than through oral
capsules." Natural. Not a banner.

### URL slugs

Format: `kebab-case`, primary keyword front-loaded, maximum 60 characters,
no dates, no stop words ("a," "the," "and") unless they're part of the
search term.

Good: `what-is-an-actoprotector`
Good: `bromantane-dopamine-synthesis-mechanism`
Good: `modafinil-long-term-cognitive-effects`
Bad: `a-comprehensive-guide-to-the-fascinating-world-of-actoprotectors`
Bad: `journal-article-2026-07-10-bromantane`

## Writing mechanics

### First person — when and how

Use first person where it adds precision about whose observation this is.

NOT: "Users report improved focus"
YES: "The effect that keeps surfacing in forum reports — and I've read
     enough threads on this to know when a pattern is noise versus
     signal — is reduced cognitive friction rather than increased
     raw speed. That's a different thing."

NOT: "Research has shown bromantane upregulates dopamine synthesis"
YES: "What the 2007 Kudrin paper actually shows — worth reading Table 3
     directly — is increased tyrosine hydroxylase expression in
     dopaminergic neurons. That's upstream of dopamine itself.
     The distinction matters for understanding why this isn't a stimulant."

NOT: "It is recommended to start with a lower dose"
YES: "Start with less. The bad-experience reports are almost always
     from people who went straight to the top of the range."

Appropriate first-person markers for this voice:
- "I went back to the original paper because the secondary summaries
  were all paraphrasing the same abstract and missing this..."
- "I've watched this argument run on forums for two years. Here's
  where I think it actually lands."
- "The number that stopped me in the data was..."
- "I don't know why this gets ignored in most nootropics coverage."
- "Honestly, I was skeptical about this until I read the followup."

### Technical credibility

Use specific details naturally. Not as a checklist.

- Name the paper: "The 2007 Kudrin paper" not "a study."
- State the methodology limitation: "The primary endpoint was
  physician-assessed symptom reduction, not an objective cognitive
  test. That's the methodological weakness."
- Distinguish mechanism from effect: "Tyrosine hydroxylase upregulation
  is the mechanism. Whether that produces the subjective motivation
  effect — that's inferred, not directly measured."
- Note bioavailability issues when relevant: "Sublingual delivery matters
  here because oral bromantane has inconsistent first-pass absorption.
  The format isn't aesthetic."
- Be specific about study populations: "This was n=40, all male, mean
  age 34, one clinical site. The effect size was large but the sample
  is small. Both things are true."

### Skepticism

Every article must contain at least one of:

1. A limitation of the evidence most coverage glosses over
2. A competing interpretation of the same data
3. A direct comparison where the article's subject loses or ties
4. An honest acknowledgment of uncertainty: "I don't have a clean
   answer here"

The skepticism should be textured, not formulaic. Not "more research is
needed" as a paragraph-ender. Say what specific evidence would actually
move the answer, and whether that evidence is likely to come.

Patterns that work:
- "The Reddit reports are all over the place on this. I take the strong
  positives as seriously as the strong negatives — which is not very."
- "This is a rat study. A well-designed one, from a credible lab.
  Still a rat study."
- "Modafinil has twenty years of human data. Bromantane has a handful
  of Russian clinical studies and several years of community self-reports.
  Those are not equivalent datasets, and one of them is still more useful
  for your decision."
- If data is conflicting, show the conflict. Don't pick the version
  that supports the conclusion.

## Forbidden words and phrases

Never under any circumstances:
- "game-changer," "revolutionary," "breakthrough," "cutting-edge"
- "delve," "tapestry," "multifaceted," "nuanced approach"
- "It's worth noting," "Notably," "Importantly,"
- "In today's world," "In recent years," "In today's fast-paced world"
- "The good news is," "The bad news is"
- "At the end of the day"
- "This begs the question"
- "Comprehensive," "robust"
- "leverage" used as a verb
- Sentences opening with "Furthermore," or "Moreover,"
- "In summary" before the final paragraph
- "Evidence-based" applied to NORA products without naming the specific
  evidence immediately after — the phrase earns its place only when
  the citation follows it

## MDX components

**`<StudyCite pmid="..." source="...">`**

For setting off a specific paper finding that's central to the argument.
Not for every citation — inline reference ("Kudrin et al., 2007") is
fine for most. Use `StudyCite` when the finding deserves visual weight.
The `pmid` attribute creates a PubMed link. The `source` attribute gives
the formatted citation line.

```mdx
<StudyCite pmid="21322821" source="Parfitt et al. · Ladasten RCT · n=728 · 2011">
The largest bromantane clinical dataset found a 76% response rate
on asthenic symptom reduction at 28 days. Primary endpoint was
physician-assessed — not an objective cognitive measure. That's
the limitation that most citations of this trial omit.
</StudyCite>
```

**`<ForumVoice source="...">`**

For direct community language that illustrates real user experience.
Not product testimonials — observations from forums that show what
the actual experience looks like. Optional `source`.

```mdx
<ForumVoice source="r/Nootropics, 2022">
I kept waiting for the crash that never came. I'm still not sure
what to make of that.
</ForumVoice>
```

Do not use `<StudyCite>` for marketing claims. Do not use `<ForumVoice>`
for invented quotes or composite characters.

Citation integrity and research workflow

The non-negotiable rule

Never put a pmid attribute in <StudyCite> unless you have verified
through PubMed that:


The PMID exists
The authors match what you believe them to be
The paper studies what you claim it studies


Fabricated or misattributed citations are worse than no citations.
A confident-looking <StudyCite> block with a wrong PMID destroys
credibility more thoroughly than an honest "I can't point to a
verifiable source for this."

The four-category system

Before writing any claim that requires support, classify it:

Category A — PubMed-verified, directly relevant
The paper exists in PubMed, the authors and title are confirmed, and the
abstract directly supports the claim being made.
→ Use <StudyCite pmid="..." source="...">. Include DOI in source field.

Category B — PubMed-verified, adjacent
The paper exists but studies something related, not exactly the claim.
For example: a toxicology study in rats that incidentally documents
dopaminergic effects, being cited for mechanism data.
→ Use <StudyCite pmid="..." source="..."> but write the block honestly —
describe what the paper actually studied, not what you wish it showed.

Category C — Non-indexed primary source
The mechanism or finding is documented in Soviet-era or Russian-language
pharmacological literature that isn't in PubMed. The claim is plausible
and referenced in secondary sources, but you can't hand the reader a
link to verify it.
→ No <StudyCite>. Write inline with explicit framing:
"This is documented in Soviet-era pharmacological literature that
predates standard English-language indexing. I can't provide a PubMed
link for verification."
→ If a secondary English-language review cites the primary, cite the
review and acknowledge the chain.

Category D — Unverifiable
The claim exists somewhere but you cannot trace it to a source.
→ Remove the claim or rewrite it as explicit uncertainty:
"This appears in secondary sources but I haven't been able to trace
it to a verifiable primary paper."

The research workflow before writing

For every article, before drafting, run this sequence:

Step 1 — Search PubMed
Search for: compound name, mechanism term, compound + mechanism combined.
Example queries:


bromantane pharmacology
bromantane dopamine
actoprotector review


Note what exists and what doesn't. Absence from PubMed is itself
information worth acknowledging in the article.

Step 2 — Pull metadata on any PMID you plan to cite
Don't rely on memory or secondary descriptions of papers. Use PubMed
to confirm authors, journal, publication year, and that the abstract
actually supports your claim.

Step 3 — Check what the paper actually measured
A toxicology study in rats that documents behavioral effects "associated
with central dopamine stimulation" is not the same as a mechanistic study
of tyrosine hydroxylase expression. Both may be real and useful. They are
different claims. Use the right framing.

Step 4 — Map claims to categories
Before writing, know which category each citation falls into. This
determines how you write the surrounding text, not just the StudyCite
block.

Step 5 — Write the epistemic qualifier into the text
For Category C claims especially: the limitation is not a footnote,
it's part of the argument. "This mechanism is documented in literature
I can't verify directly" is more trustworthy than a citation block that
turns out to be wrong. The reader deserves to know how confident to be.

Known verified sources (as of July 2026)

PMID 21322821 — Voznesenskaia TG, Fokina NM, Iakhno NN
"Treatment of asthenic disorders... ladasten" · Zh Nevrol Psikhiatr · 2010
n=728, 28 days, 76% responder rate, 3% adverse events. No DOI.
Use for: Ladasten clinical evidence, safety profile, asthenia indication.

PMID 12124651 — Iezhitsa IN, Spasov AA, Bugaeva LI, Morozov IS
"Toxic effect of single treatment with bromantane..." · Bull Exp Biol Med · 2002
DOI: 10.1023/a:1016206306875
Rat study, wide dose range. Documents central dopaminergic stimulation
at therapeutic doses, wide safety margin (LD behavior >600mg/kg).
Use for: dopaminergic mechanism (with Category B framing), safety margin.

PMID 40223246 — Jędrejko K, Catlin O, Faiss R, Pokrywka A
"A Review of Hypoxen Pharmacology..." · Drug Test Anal · 2025
DOI: 10.1002/dta.3887
Defines actoprotectors as a class, lists bromantane as synthetic actoprotector,
notes the body of knowledge from Russian Military Medical Academy.
Use for: actoprotector class definition, historical framing.

What is NOT in PubMed (as of July 2026)


Bromantane + tyrosine hydroxylase upregulation: no indexed paper found
Bromantane + dopamine synthesis mechanism: no indexed English paper found
Ladasten cognitive enhancement in healthy subjects: no indexed trial found


These claims may be real — they appear in secondary sources and are
mechanistically plausible — but they are Category C until a primary
source is located and verified.

## Final checklist before publishing

Read the draft and ask:

1. Is there any sentence that sounds like it was written to sell something?
   Rewrite or cut.
2. Does the primary keyword appear in the title, first 100 words, and
   at least one H2?
3. Is the meta description under 160 characters and does it directly
   answer the search intent?
4. Are all sections the same length? They shouldn't be.
5. Is there at least one genuine limitation of the evidence acknowledged?
6. Is there at least one moment of honest uncertainty?
7. Could any paragraph be dropped unchanged into a generic AI response?
   If yes — rewrite it.
8. Does the first sentence read like a person starting a conversation,
   not like an article introduction?
9. If the article touches bromantane or NeuroDrive — is the commercial
   relationship acknowledged once?
10. Are there 2–3 internal links to other journal articles, and one
    contextual link to the product page where it genuinely fits?

## Content taxonomy

### Four categories

**`mechanism`** — How specific compounds or physiological pathways
actually work. Not recommendations — understanding the biology.
Reader is trying to understand before deciding. Lead with the
definition or core mechanism in paragraph one. Google rewards this
for "what is X" and "how does X work" queries.

**`research`** — Reading and interpreting specific trials or bodies of
evidence. Reader has seen a claim and wants to know if it holds.
Lead with the verdict, then the evidence, then the limits. Targeting
"does X work," "X evidence review," "X clinical trial results."

**`perspective`** — Argued editorial positions on the nootropics and
biohacking landscape. No citations required — these are analytical
takes, not literature reviews. Reader wants a position, not a survey.
Targeting "why X doesn't work," "the problem with X," "X is wrong."

**`guide`** — Practical frameworks for decision-making. Not product
recommendations — frameworks the reader applies themselves. Give
the actionable answer early, depth after. Targeting "how to X,"
"X protocol," "X framework."

```yaml
title: "Primary keyword front-loaded — differentiating clause after colon"

category: "mechanism" | "research" | "perspective" | "guide"

  - "primary keyword phrase"
  - "secondary keyword"
  - "semantic variation"
  - "one more if natural"
relatedSlugs:
  - "other-article-slug"
  - "another-article-slug"

```

The `description` field is the meta description for search engines and
the excerpt shown in article cards. Write it to answer the search query,
not to intrigue. "How the actoprotector class differs from stimulants —
and why that distinction matters for tolerance and dependency risk"
outperforms "The fascinating compound class that Western nootropics
forgot about."
